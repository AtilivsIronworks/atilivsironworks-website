When a Sword Becomes a Knife: Lessons from a Failed Forge Weld

An account of an unsuccessful Viking Age sword reconstruction, the challenges of forge-welding wrought iron and high-carbon steel, and the subsequent transformation of the material into a Scandinavian long knife.

Introduction

Not every project that enters the forge comes out as originally intended. This is especially true when experimenting with historical construction methods and unfamiliar materials. The knife presented in this article began as an attempt to produce a Viking Age sword with a wrought-iron core and two high-carbon steel edges. The intended sword was inspired by the weapons associated with the famous Birka grave Bj 581, with a hilt based on Petersen's Type V classification.

The project ultimately failed during the construction of the blade's point. Repeated attempts to complete the fish-mouth weld resulted in excessive material loss, making the original sword impossible to finish. Rather than discard the remaining billet, I chose to repurpose it into a Scandinavian long knife, taking inspiration from archaeological examples associated with the eastern Scandinavian and Rus cultural sphere.

The purpose of this article is to document the original historical inspiration, examine the technical difficulties encountered during construction, explain the decisions that led to the change in design, and reflect on the lessons learned.

1. Historical Inspiration: Birka Grave Bj 581

The original sword was intended as a gift. A friend approached me about making a sword for his squire, which he hoped to present to her when she became a knight within the Society for Creative Anachronism (SCA). I wanted the sword to be both historically meaningful and a challenging piece of craftsmanship. After considering the intended recipient and the occasion, we settled on a sword inspired by the burial assemblage of Birka grave Bj 581.

Located on the island of Björkö in Sweden, Birka was an important Viking Age trading settlement. Grave Bj 581 is among its most extensively discussed burials, owing to the wealth of weapons and military equipment recovered from it. The burial contained a sword, axe, fighting knife, two spears, two shields, and a collection of arrows, along with two horses and gaming equipment. These objects contributed to its long-standing interpretation as the grave of a high-status warrior.

The individual was originally assumed to have been male, largely because of the martial equipment associated with the burial. However, osteological examination and subsequent genetic analysis established that the buried individual was biologically female (Hedenstierna-Jonson et al. 2017; Price et al. 2019). The interpretation of the individual's precise social and military role remains a matter of scholarly discussion. Nevertheless, the burial provides an important example of a woman interred with an extensive collection of weapons during the Viking Age, making it a particularly interesting source of inspiration for a sword intended to commemorate a woman's elevation to knighthood.

1.1. Petersen Types V and E: The Attribution Problem

Before beginning the project, we were aware that the attribution of the sword associated with Bj 581 had been debated. Jan Petersen's sword typology, published in 1919, primarily classified Viking Age swords according to the morphology and construction of their hilts rather than the shape of their blades. The classification system remains widely used in the study of Viking Age swords, although individual finds are sometimes difficult to assign conclusively.

Type V is characterized by a two-part pommel with a generally rounded, three-lobed upper portion. The transitions between the lobes can be indistinct, and the guards and pommels frequently feature geometric decoration, including stepped patterns executed in non-ferrous metals (Petersen 1919; Viskupič 2025). Type E represents another family of decorated hilts, including examples with hollow components and distinctive pitted or pierced decoration, sometimes associated with decorative wirework.

The distinction is relevant to Bj 581 because different researchers have assigned particular surviving swords to different Birka graves. In his 2014 catalogue of Viking Age Swedish swords, Fedir Androshchuk attributed a Type V sword to Bj 581 and a Type E sword to Bj 524. Subsequent reassessment of the grave assemblages has challenged that attribution. Tomáš Vlasatý's discussion of incorrectly labelled swords, followed by Michal Viskupič's examination of Type V swords, argues that the fragmented Type V sword belongs to Bj 524 rather than Bj 581.

Research presented by Linda Wåhlander and Peter Johnsson in Buried at Birka (2023) discusses the sword associated with Bj 581 as Type E, taking into account the original excavation drawings and surviving material. There is an additional catalogue complication, however. The Swedish History Museum currently lists the sword identified with Bj 581, inventory number 263195_HST, as Petersen Type W. A separate sword registered as Type E, inventory number 263051_HST, has an uncertain grave attribution according to the museum's catalogue. Consequently, the historical disagreement is not merely a question of whether a particular pommel resembles Type V or Type E. It also concerns the attribution and identification of individual archaeological objects.

For our reconstruction, we were aware of the Type V and Type E disagreement before beginning. We ultimately decided on a Petersen Type V hilt because we preferred its appearance. This was a deliberate aesthetic choice, not an assertion that Type V was unquestionably the sword buried in Bj 581. The intended sword was therefore a historically inspired interpretation rather than an attempt to reproduce the complete grave find exactly.

2. The Proposed Blade Construction

The original plan called for a double-edged blade made from three longitudinal bars. Two bars of modern 1084 high-carbon steel would form the cutting edges, while a wrought-iron bar would occupy the center. The resulting construction would place hardenable steel where cutting performance was required while incorporating wrought iron into the body of the blade.

Although composite construction is well documented in early medieval blades, the specific arrangement chosen for this project should not be regarded as a demonstrated reconstruction of the metallurgical structure of the Bj 581 sword. It was a construction experiment based on historical techniques and intended to challenge my existing abilities. I had previously completed a sword with a high-layer-count random pattern-welded blade; however, I had never successfully produced a sword-length billet with this particular combination of wrought iron and steel. I had also only completed one successful fish-mouth weld before attempting this project. My confidence going into the work was relatively low, but gaining experience with more complicated constructions was one of my primary motivations.

2.1. The Difficulties of Wrought Iron

Working wrought iron presents challenges that are not always encountered to the same extent with modern homogeneous steel. Wrought iron commonly contains elongated slag inclusions created during its manufacture and subsequent working. These can contribute to a fibrous appearance and influence how the material behaves under the hammer. In my experience, wrought iron tends to move more readily than the adjacent high-carbon steel at the temperatures I use, and it can tear along its grain when worked too cold or too aggressively.

Maintaining the alignment of the three bars while drawing out the billet was therefore a major concern. I needed to move sufficient material to produce a full-length sword while preserving the integrity of the welds and avoiding excessive distortion between the iron core and the steel edges. This required repeated heats, controlled hammer blows, and considerable patience. Unfortunately, another problem developed as the blade approached its intended length.

3. Insufficient Starting Material

The most consequential mistake in the project occurred before the fish-mouth weld was attempted. I underestimated the amount of material needed to produce the finished sword. I believe the original billet began at approximately 1.5 pounds (0.68 kg), although this figure is based on my recollection rather than a surviving weight record. During forging, the billet was progressively drawn out toward the desired sword length. By the time the blade approached that length, the material at the point had become less than 3 mm thick.

For a finished blade, such a thickness might not be unusual, depending on its geometry. However, the tip of this sword was not finished, and it still required a substantial forge weld. The intended fish-mouth construction involved bringing the steel edges together around the wrought-iron core and welding them closed to form the point. At less than 3 mm thickness, the material had become extremely difficult for me to control at welding temperature. The problem was not simply reaching welding heat, but retaining sufficient heat and maintaining the shape of the joint long enough to establish a successful weld.

3.1. The Failed Fish-Mouth Weld

My approach was to heat the prepared point and begin welding from the base of the joint toward the tip. Under normal circumstances, this progression should help bring the contacting surfaces together while allowing the smith to work toward the end of the joint. In this case, however, the material was too thin for me to manage effectively. As soon as I began working the joint on the anvil, the thin steel tended to fold over instead of remaining in the intended position. Lighter hammer blows did not resolve the problem, and the anvil rapidly drew heat from the small volume of material, reducing the time available to work the weld.

After an unsuccessful attempt, I cut away the damaged section, prepared the remaining material, and tried again. I repeated this process approximately three or four times, making no substantial changes to the welding method between attempts. Each time the failed end was removed, the remaining material was somewhat thicker, but the overall length of the sword was reduced. Eventually, the cost of continuing became unavoidable. Every attempt removed more material, and there was no longer enough remaining to complete the intended sword. The project had reached the point where I needed to choose between abandoning it entirely or changing its purpose.

4. Failure and Reconsideration

The failure was particularly disappointing because the sword had been intended for friends. I knew that the construction was within the realm of possibility, and I believed I could eventually develop the skill to complete it. However, that did not change the fact that I had failed to deliver the sword I had agreed to make. There was frustration in recognizing that a mistake in planning had limited my ability to succeed, particularly when the sword was intended to mark an important occasion for someone else.

Looking back, I believe there were two approaches that could have substantially improved my chances. The first would have been to begin with considerably more material. Rather than approximately 1.5 pounds, I would now consider starting with at least 3 pounds (1.36 kg), and possibly more, depending on the intended dimensions and anticipated material losses. The second would have been to complete the fish-mouth weld earlier in the forging sequence, before the material at the point became too thin.

Since completing this project, I have found that my present skills and equipment are better suited to performing this operation when the material is approximately 7–10 mm thick. This is a personal workshop guideline, not a universal requirement for historical fish-mouth welding. Because the planned blade did not contain twisted pattern-welded rods, closing the tip earlier would have been a practical option. A blade containing a more complicated patterned core could require a different sequence to preserve the desired arrangement. These were lessons I had learned at the expense of the original sword. However, the remaining material presented another possibility.

5. Reconsidering the Value of the Material

My first inclination was to consign the failed billet to the scrap pile. I had already spent considerable time preparing, welding, and drawing out the material, only to discover that the original project was no longer viable. However, as I considered what remained, I began approaching the problem from a perspective that I had not given sufficient attention before: the value of the material itself.

Modern bladesmiths generally have access to commercially produced steel in standardized sizes and compositions. If we need more material, we can purchase it. Historical iron production required considerably more effort, involving the acquisition of suitable raw materials, preparation of fuel, operation of a smelting furnace, consolidation of the bloom, and refinement of the resulting material into workable stock. The exact availability and cost of iron varied considerably between regions, periods, and communities, but producing it still represented substantial labor and resources.

I began asking myself what a historical smith might have done with a failed billet that still contained a substantial quantity of usable iron. Would it have been discarded simply because the intended sword could no longer be completed, or might the material have been repurposed for another object? I cannot claim that a particular Viking Age smith would have transformed a failed sword into a knife in the same way I did, but considering the labor invested in producing the material changed how I viewed the remaining billet.

The billet was still sound through much of its length. It was no longer suitable for the sword I had intended, but there was enough material to produce a long knife. As I had already wanted a Scandinavian long knife for my Norse, Rus, and Varangian reenactment kit, the decision became relatively straightforward. The sword would not be completed, but the material would not be wasted.

6. The Scandinavian Long Knife

The new project drew inspiration from Scandinavian and eastern Baltic long knives of the Viking Age, particularly examples associated with Sweden and the eastern trade routes. My research was informed by Tomáš Vlasatý's examination of the development of Scandinavian long knives, as well as his catalogue of wire-wrapped knife handles dating from the ninth through twelfth centuries. These studies document a variety of blade lengths, handle constructions, and decorative methods across Scandinavia and neighboring regions. The surviving evidence also demonstrates that there was no single standardized construction used for every Viking Age long knife.

For this project, I wanted a relatively narrow, single-edged blade with a wooden grip, wire decoration, and a suspension ring at the end of the handle. The finished knife was not intended as an exact copy of a particular archaeological specimen. Instead, it combined features informed by several surviving examples with the unusual blade construction inherited from the failed sword.

6.1. Reforging the Billet

Reworking the remaining material presented its own difficulties. The billet still consisted of two steel bars surrounding a wrought-iron center. This was a departure from the iron-backed, steel-edged construction documented in some historical knives, because the finished blade would contain high-carbon steel along both its cutting edge and its spine.

The primary concern during forging was maintaining the integrity of the original welds. I needed to reduce the stock into the narrower proportions of the knife without allowing the wrought iron to tear or separate from the adjacent steel. The solution was not particularly complicated, although it required patience and careful control. I worked at sufficiently high temperatures, avoided excessive deformation in a single heat, and took my time. Rather than forcing the material to move more quickly, I focused on controlling how it moved.

The resulting blade finished at 325 mm in length, with a width of 27 mm at the shoulder and a spine approximately 5 mm thick. The reduced spine thickness was partly a consequence of the material lost during the unsuccessful sword construction and subsequent reforging.

6.2. Handle Construction and Wire Wrapping

The handle was made from oak, with seven recessed bands of brass wire and an iron suspension ring at the end. The decision to use seven wire bands was influenced by surviving examples discussed by Vlasatý, including knives from Timerevo grave 348 and Birka grave 1130. I used 1.0 mm brass wire, a relatively substantial diameter compared with the 0.5–0.6 mm wire commonly documented in archaeological examples, although thicker examples are also known. The finished grip was 155 mm long.

I was pleased with the appearance of the handle, particularly how cleanly the recessed wire bands sat within the oak and how comfortable the grip felt in my hand. Nevertheless, I would make several changes if I were to repeat the work. The handle could benefit from a slightly more oval cross-section, and I would also reduce its overall length. These are details I am continuing to consider as I work on the sheath.

Following conversations with Götz Breitenbücher of Götz Ironworks, I became aware of an additional construction detail I had not incorporated. Rather than producing separate wire bands, as I had done, the method he described employed a continuous length of wire, with twisted connecting sections running between the individual wraps. Comparable connected wrapping arrangements are documented among surviving early medieval knife handles (Vlasatý 2021). Although I am happy with the finished result, this is one of the details I would like to explore in a future reconstruction.

7. Lessons from the Reconstruction

Although the original sword was unsuccessful, the project changed several aspects of my approach to bladesmithing. The most immediate lesson concerned estimating the amount of material required for a particular construction. There is a balance between starting with enough stock to account for forging losses and using an unnecessarily large quantity of material. For complex laminated constructions, I now prefer to give myself a greater margin for error.

My approach to forge welding has also become more deliberate. I pay closer attention to the cleanliness of the surfaces being joined, maintaining sufficient welding heat, and allowing the billet to heat thoroughly before beginning work. When drawing out composite billets, particularly those containing wrought iron, I also try to work all four sides rather than repeatedly deforming the material from a single direction. Overlapping heats and avoiding excessive deformation have become important parts of that process. None of these practices eliminates the possibility of failure, but they have helped me develop better control over the material.

Perhaps the most important lesson, however, has been patience. It is easy to become focused on the intended finished object and begin pushing the material faster than the process allows. Sometimes the better approach is to slow down, examine what is happening, and reconsider the next step. That is especially true when working with unfamiliar constructions, where the behavior of the material may not match what we initially expect.

Conclusion

The original goal of this project was to produce a Viking Age sword for a friend's squire. That goal was not achieved. The combination of insufficient starting material, an unsuccessful fish-mouth weld, and repeated attempts to correct the problem ultimately made the intended sword impossible to complete. However, the remaining material was not wasted. Instead, it became a Scandinavian long knife that allowed me to investigate a different historical form and develop additional skills in forging, handle construction, and wire decoration.

There are aspects of the finished knife I would change. Its construction is not representative of every historical example, the proportions are not exact matches for the archaeological comparisons, and several details could be improved. Nevertheless, I am proud of the result, not because the original attempt succeeded, but because I was able to adapt to the failure and produce something beautiful from what remained.

More complicated constructions will inevitably present greater challenges, and I expect to encounter unsuccessful welds and abandoned approaches again as I continue learning. The important thing is to understand what went wrong, apply those lessons, and approach the next project with better knowledge than before. There is a familiar saying among bladesmiths: "Bladesmiths never fail, they only make shorter knives." This project gave me a rather literal example of that idea. While I still intend to return to the challenge of making that sword, I am glad the material found another purpose.
