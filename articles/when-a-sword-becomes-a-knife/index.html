When a Sword Becomes a Knife

Lessons from a failed forge weld, a Viking Age sword inspired by Birka grave Bj 581, and the Scandinavian long knife that came out of it.

This project began with a sword I wanted to make for someone else. It ended with a knife I never intended to forge. Between those two points were a series of weld failures, a fair amount of lost material, and a better understanding of what I still needed to learn at the anvil.

The original plan

A friend had approached me about making a sword for his squire, to be presented when she became a knight. It was a meaningful commission, and I wanted the sword to have a historical connection appropriate to the person receiving it. We settled on a Viking Age sword inspired by a find associated with Birka grave Bj 581 in Sweden.

The grave caught our attention for an obvious reason. Bj 581 is a richly furnished burial containing a sword, axe, spear, knife, arrows and other martial equipment. The buried individual was long assumed to be male because of the weapons, but osteological examination and a 2017 genetic study identified the individual as biologically female.[1] The burial is important evidence in discussions of gender, martial identity and the limits of our assumptions about Viking Age society. It does not, by itself, allow us to reconstruct the individual's life or establish every role they held.

The original intention was a sword in the general style of Petersen Type V. We were aware that the identification of the Bj 581 sword was contested. I preferred the Type V form and we chose to follow it as a design inspiration, rather than claim to be making a precise replica of the object recovered from that grave.

Birka Bj 581: Type V or Type E?

That distinction matters. Petersen's typology groups early medieval swords largely by their hilt forms, not simply by the shapes of their blades. Types V and E can appear related at first glance, particularly in the overall outlines of their guards and pommels, but their construction and decorative details offer important evidence for separating them.

Fedir Androshchuk's 2014 catalogue proposed that a more complete sword traditionally assigned to Bj 581 should instead be associated with Bj 524, and that the fragmentary Type V sword belonged to Bj 581. Later reconsiderations have argued for the earlier archaeological association: the more complete sword with pitted decoration is from Bj 581, and the fragmentary Type V sword is from Bj 524. Tomáš Vlasatý reviewed the identification in 2022, and Michal Viskupič discussed it again in his 2025 study of Petersen Type V.[2][3]

In that interpretation, the pitted decorative treatment supports a Type E identification for the sword from Bj 581, while the remaining components of the Bj 524 sword fit Type V. The disagreement involves the attribution of finds to their graves as well as the classification of hilt details. It is not enough to call a sword Type V because it has a broadly similar pommel.

For this project, the decision remained a personal design choice. Our intended sword drew inspiration from the burial and from Viking Age hilt traditions, but it was not an exact archaeological reconstruction of the Bj 581 sword. Making that distinction clear is part of the documentation.

Building the billet

The planned blade used three longitudinal bars: wrought iron in the center, with 1084 high-carbon steel on either side. The idea was to forge a double-edged sword with the steel forming both cutting edges. At the point, the two steel bars would meet around the iron core in a fish-mouth weld. The initial starting assembly was only about 1.5 pounds. In retrospect, that left too little margin for a full sword once the losses from welding, forging and cleanup were taken into account.

The first part of the work went as planned. I prepared the bars, held them in alignment, brought the assembly to welding heat, and consolidated them into a single billet. With the long welds set, attention turned to forming and closing the tip. That was where the project began to come apart.

The fish-mouth weld: first failure

A fish-mouth joint relies on bringing the two sides of the point together and welding them into a sound, continuous tip. In theory the geometry is straightforward. In practice, the tip can become a difficult area to manage because the stock is narrow, the surfaces must meet properly, and it is easy to lose the heat needed for a good weld.

On my first attempt, the point did not close cleanly. Once I ground back the surface to inspect it, there was a visible opening in the weld. I could not treat that as a cosmetic defect. It was a failure of the joint at the very end of what was supposed to become a double-edged blade.

Trying again, and again

I went back to the forge to correct the problem. A second attempt still showed an opening at the tip. I cleaned up the area, reheated, and tried to establish the weld again. The third photographed attempt also failed. Each round meant more heat, more hammering, and more material removed when the damaged area was ground back for inspection.

The photographs document three unsuccessful tip welds. They show the physical result, not every contributing variable. My best explanation is that I had allowed the point to become too thin while trying to form the joint. The narrow tip cooled more quickly than the heavier section behind it, making it difficult to achieve and maintain the heat required across the seam. Fit-up and the repeated reworking may also have mattered, but without a controlled test I cannot assign a single cause with certainty.

Working the wrought iron introduced another difficulty. Unlike modern homogeneous steel, historic wrought material can contain slag layers and may separate or tear when the temperature and hammer work are poorly matched to the material. I saw evidence of tearing during the attempts to salvage the point. That did not mean the whole billet was worthless, but it did mean I needed to stop thinking of it as a sword billet that could simply be repaired one more time.

A different blade from the same steel

By the time I accepted that the sword was not going to work, too much stock had been lost to preserve the original intended proportions. Rather than keep chasing the weld or throw the whole billet into the scrap pile, I cut away the failed section and evaluated what sound material remained. There was still enough to make something useful.

I reforged the remaining billet into a single-edged Scandinavian long knife. The new form did not require the same closing weld at the tip. I formed a fresh point and worked the blade into a long, narrow profile. It was not a planned transition, but it gave me a reason to research another part of the Viking Age blade tradition.

The eventual knife retained the unusual three-bar construction inherited from the sword, with steel at the edge and spine and wrought iron between them. That is a consequence of the salvage process, not a claim that all Scandinavian long knives were made that way. The finished piece received an oak handle with recessed brass wire bands and an iron suspension ring, informed by comparative long-knife finds rather than copied from one archaeological original.[4]

There is a separate portfolio and construction record for the finished long knife, with its final measurements, heat treatment, handle work and photographs. This article is about how the original sword failed and why I chose not to abandon the steel.

What I learned

The first lesson was to start with enough material. A billet that looks substantial at the beginning can become surprisingly small after scale loss, repeated weld preparations, grinding and forging. On a project involving several different pieces of metal, I need to account for those losses before I begin rather than try to recover the missing length later.

The second was to pay closer attention to the geometry of a weld before applying more heat. Once the point was too thin, another trip to the forge was not automatically going to solve the problem. I needed more mass where the weld was being made, a better fit, and enough control to keep the whole joint at an appropriate working temperature. Repeating the same operation without addressing those conditions only cost me more material.

The third lesson was knowing when to change the plan. I wanted to make the sword, and it was disappointing to admit that it was not going to happen from that billet. But the remaining steel was still useful. Turning it into a long knife did not erase the mistakes. It preserved the work already invested in the material and gave me a new set of skills to practice.

I do not consider this a successful sword build. The sword failed. The knife succeeded as a separate project, and both outcomes belong in the record. That is why I am documenting the bad welds alongside the finished work. A photograph of a completed blade can show what I made; photographs of the failures help explain what it took to get there.
