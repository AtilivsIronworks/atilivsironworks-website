Selected work

Anglo-Saxon Broken Back Seax

Coppergate, York · Multi-bar pattern weld

The Project

This broken-back seax explores Anglo-Scandinavian composite blade construction through a wrought-iron spine, an opposing-twist pattern-welded core, and a filed, interlocking wolf-tooth weld joining wrought iron to the high-carbon steel edge. The finished blade has an approximately 5 mm spine, an African blackwood handle, copper fittings and wirework, and a decorated leather scabbard.

Historical Inspiration

The blade's profile was informed by knives recovered at 16–22 Coppergate, York, particularly catalogue numbers 2764 and 2765 in Patrick Ottaway's published study. Both belong to the site's Period 3 assemblage. These are comparisons for form, not direct models for the elaborate pattern-welded construction of this seax.

Knife 2764 has an 83 mm blade, 17 mm wide and 5 mm thick, with an angled back and a groove on each blade face. Knife 2765 has a 68 mm blade, 13 mm wide and 4 mm thick, with a similarly angled back, chamfered edges, and traces of a wooden handle on the tang. Both are illustrated in Ottaway's Fig. 227.

Metallographic analysis of knife 2765 found a composite construction with an iron back and welded steel cutting edge, including carbon diffusion at the weld and a hardened martensitic edge. More elaborate Coppergate pattern welding is documented on knife 2756 (Plate XLIX). This project combines lessons from several archaeological examples; it should not be understood as an exact replica of any one find.

Construction

Two separate nine-layer billets of alternating 1075 and 15N20 were forge-welded and twisted in opposite directions. They were arranged together to produce a mirrored, herringbone-like core pattern, then joined with wrought iron to form the main assembly.

For the wolf-tooth joint, I filed matching teeth into both the wrought iron and the 1084 steel, fitted the two components together, and forge-welded them. I chose filing rather than forging the teeth into shape because my hammer control at the time was not yet consistent enough for the result I wanted. The etched joint reveals the interlocking pattern.

Once all components were welded together, I cut the blade profile from the billet rather than forging the final shape. This was a deliberate choice to avoid distorting the twisted core and wolf-tooth patterns. The blade was then cleaned, ground, heat-treated, polished, and etched to reveal its construction.

The planned construction required 22 forge-welding operations: nine for each twisted billet (18), one joining the twists, one attaching the wrought-iron spine, one making the wolf-tooth joint, and one joining that assembly to the remainder of the blade. This is a workshop count of operations, not a claim that each individual layer interface was welded in a separate heat. Delaminations and restarts added to the practical challenge of the build.

Dimensions: Spine thickness approximately 5 mm. Final blade length and width were not recorded before the finished knife went to its new owner; archaeological dimensions above belong to the original Coppergate finds.

At a glance
Inspiration

Anglo-Scandinavian broken-back knives from Coppergate, York, especially finds 2764 and 2765; interpreted as a composite-blade study, not a direct replica.

Blade Dimensions

Spine thickness approximately 5 mm. Blade length, shoulder width, and other final measurements were not recorded.

Work Materials

Blade: wrought-iron spine, opposing 1075/15N20 pattern-welded bars, wrought iron and 1084 high-carbon steel at the wolf-tooth edge. Handle: African blackwood. Non-ferrous fittings: copper and wirework.
