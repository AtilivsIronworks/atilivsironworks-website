Roman-Inspired Utility Knife

A practical blade made in trade for a custom banner, and an early experiment with hand-carving bone.

Finished-object photographs are being added

The Project

This knife was finished on September 4, 2025, as part of a trade for a custom banner. It was a relatively simple project, but it gave me an opportunity to experiment with something I had wanted to learn for a long time: carving decoration by hand.

I have always been interested in the work that goes into decorated weapons and tools, from Roman pugio fittings to the detailed hilts of Viking Age swords. I wanted to start developing some of those skills, and a small utility knife seemed like a good place to begin.

Historical Inspiration

This knife was not based on one archaeological original. I had been looking through photographs of Roman knives on different websites and in museum collections, and I brought together features I liked into my own design. It is a Roman-inspired working knife, rather than a measured reconstruction of a surviving piece.

The decoration was part of that exploration. More elaborate Roman daggers and early medieval sword hilts were the kinds of projects that drew me toward carving and inlay work, even though this knife itself is much simpler. I wanted to understand what it takes to make clean decorative lines before trying anything more involved.

Construction

The Blade and Ring

I forged the blade from 1084 steel, working the knife by hand, including the ring at the end of the handle. The finished blade measured 3 inches long, ¾ inch wide at the shoulder, and 3 mm thick. The handle measured 3½ inches.

I spent extra time normalizing the steel because of the change in cross-section around the ring. I have had knives crack or break where the material transitions from a large section to a much smaller one, and I was mindful of that while working this blade. The exact heat-treatment temperatures were not preserved in my notes.

The Hand-Carved Bone Handle

The handle was made from a cow femur I found while hiking. I used two bone scales, secured to the handle with pins. Bone was not a material I had worked with much, and this was one of my first serious attempts to carve a decorative pattern into it.

All the carving was done by hand using graver tools. I laid out groups of closely spaced straight lines running in different directions, separated by narrow bands. I was trying to practice control and consistency rather than create an elaborate design for its own sake.

Challenges and Lessons

The forging itself was fairly straightforward. The carving was not. I learned quickly that cutting a straight line by hand is much harder than it looks, especially when working across the grain of the bone. I broke several scales along the way and had to start over.

That was frustrating, but it was also the reason I wanted to make the knife. Carving and inlay work were skills I admired in historical metalwork, and the only way to develop them was to try, make mistakes, and learn how the material responded.

Final Reflections

Looking back, I see this as one of my early steps toward more decorative work. It wasn't a complicated knife and I didn't encounter anything unusual while forging it, but the hand-carved grip pushed me into unfamiliar territory.

I still want to improve my carving and inlay work. The richly decorated fittings of Roman pugiones and the inlaid and ornamented hilts of Viking Age swords are both part of what makes historical bladesmithing interesting to me. This little knife was a chance to begin learning the control those larger projects demand.

The construction and measurements above are recorded from my recollections of the project. No single historical knife is identified as the direct model, and no archaeological reconstruction claim is made.

Inspiration

Blade Dimensions

Blade length 3 in (76.2 mm); width at shoulder ¾ in (19.1 mm); blade thickness 3 mm; handle length 3½ in (88.9 mm). Overall length, weight, and point of balance were not recorded.

Work Materials

Blade: hand-forged 1084 steel with a forged ring at the handle end. Handle: two cow-femur bone scales, hand-carved using gravers and secured with pins. Pin material was not recorded.
