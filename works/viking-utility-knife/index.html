Viking Age Inspired Utility Knife

Composite wrought iron and steel blade, silver-wrapped red oak handle, and a hand-decorated brass and leather sheath.

The Project

I made this knife as a gift for a very close friend and family member. I wanted something that drew on Viking Age utility knives, particularly examples associated with Birka, but with more detailed construction than many surviving everyday knives.

The blade ended up larger than many of the knives I was looking at. I didn't want to forge it down further and risk losing the twist pattern running through the blade. That was a deliberate choice, and part of why this is an inspired piece rather than a direct reconstruction.

Historical Inspiration

I looked at several surviving Viking Age knives and their sheaths rather than copying one particular find. Many knives in this general tradition were simpler tools, and I chose to put considerably more work into this one because of who it was for.

The sheath decoration also drew from historical examples with brass fittings and stepped openwork. After finishing it, I realized I had missed important details in how some of those fittings were constructed. Those differences are explained below, rather than treating my version as an exact copy of an archaeological example.

Construction

I forge-welded the blade from four components: a wrought-iron spine, a low-layer twisted section of 1074 and 15N20, a wrought-iron middle section, and a 1084 cutting edge. I made the forge welds myself. After finishing the blade, I etched it with a commercial product called Gator Piss to bring out the different steels. I don't know the exact composition of that etchant.

The handle is red oak with a wrap of .999 fine silver wire near the blade. I wanted that simple wood-and-wire finish to complement the more elaborate pattern in the steel and the brass fittings.

The Sheath

The sheath is made from vegetable-tanned leather dyed brown, with brass fittings and a chain that I made by hand. This was my first attempt at this level of detail on a sheath. I cut, filed, chased, and shaped the brass myself using files, chasing tools, and repoussé tools.

I spent a lot of time working out the stepped edges, decorative lines, and rivets. The closer photographs show the results, including marks and small irregularities from the handwork.

Challenges and Lessons

I missed two details in the historical examples I was studying. On some surviving sheaths the stepped decoration forms enclosed openwork, with the outer edge closed rather than left open as I made mine. Many also have another non-ferrous layer behind the cutouts. That keeps the leather from showing through and creates a contrast between the metals. These features aren't universal, but I should have looked more closely before I made the fittings.

Götz Ironworks and David Stříbrný were kind enough to point this out when they saw the finished sheath. It was useful feedback. I'll change those details when I make another one.

The tiny rivets and the alignment of the chased decoration also took more work than I expected. The front turned out cleaner than the back, and a few of the holes didn't line up as neatly as I wanted. I made it work, but those are things I want to improve.

Final Reflections

This was the first sheath I've made with brasswork at this level, and I'm extremely happy with how it came together. There are mistakes I can see now and things I'll do differently next time, but I learned a great deal making it. More importantly, I was able to give someone close to me a knife that I put real time and care into.

Completed November 13, 2026. Made as a gift. No final measurements were recorded before the knife was given away. This is a modern interpretation informed by several historical examples, not a reconstruction of one identified find.
