Roman Pugio, Type III

Double-herringbone pattern welding, a brass hilt with cupped rivets, and my only successful fish-mouth weld.

The Project

I've always been fascinated by the balance of practicality and decoration in Roman weapons. The pugio is a small blade, but there is a surprising amount of work in its design, especially when a patterned blade and ornate metal grip are involved.

My Roman persona is set in the late second to early third centuries, and studying these weapons is part of how I connect that interest to my work at the forge. In my original documentation, I set out to recreate a Type III pugio while improving my pattern welding and decorative metalwork. It was as much a chance to learn as it was an attempt to make a finished dagger.

Historical Inspiration

I was drawn to the broad, waisted blade, central ridge and more elaborate grip details associated with some Roman pugiones. My early notes refer to Marco Saliola and Fabrizio Casprini's Pugio: Gladius Brevis Est and mention a comparative example from Künzing, Germany. Those references informed the direction of the project. I would describe my finished dagger as Type III-inspired rather than a measured reproduction of one archaeological find.

The double-herringbone pattern was particularly appealing. I wanted to explore opposing twists of steel and wrought iron and see how well I could carry that structure through a finished dagger. I used modern materials and tools to pursue that goal, and those choices are part of the reconstruction rather than claims about exactly how a Roman smith worked.

Research distinction: The original documentation contains a broader account of Roman pugio typology. That discussion has not been independently checked against all its cited publications, so this Works page focuses on the securely documented design intentions and my own construction record.

Construction

The Blade

My written plan specifies 1080 steel and wrought iron for the patterned core, with 1080 steel forming the cutting edges. It describes preparing and forge-welding bars, twisting two billets in opposite directions, and combining them to form the chevron-like pattern. This was one of the main technical challenges I wanted to tackle.

I worked with a propane forge, a 12-ton press, a 2×72 grinder, and hand-forging tools. The process involved building the patterned stock, shaping the blade and central ridge, grinding and finishing the surfaces, and heat treatment. My surviving notes outline the heat-treatment stage but do not provide measured temperatures or a calibrated hardness result for the finished dagger.

A Successful Fish-Mouth Weld

This dagger also has one distinction that makes it especially memorable to me: it is the only fish-mouth weld I've successfully completed. A fish-mouth weld closes a prepared opening at the point, bringing the steel together and forge-welding the joint. Later projects reminded me how unforgiving that operation can be, so this is one success I still appreciate.

I no longer have a full step-by-step record of that weld. I would rather be clear about what I remember than try to reconstruct undocumented heats, hammer work or adjustments years afterward.

The Brass Hilt

The original plan included additional brass inlay, but I ultimately concentrated on making the solid brass grip and the handmade cupped rivets seen in historical examples. Working the hilt into shape required careful fitting and finishing, and getting the domed, recessed rivets right took several attempts.

The recesses of the finished rivets are painted red. I chose that decorative treatment for this piece, not as a claim that the color reproduces an archaeological original.

Challenges and Lessons

Brasswork pushed me hardest on this project. There was more precision involved than I expected, especially in making the fittings look consistent and getting the cupped rivets to sit properly. I had to make adjustments and keep working until the shape and assembly came together.

I also learned to narrow my ambitions when I needed to. I began with ideas for more extensive inlay work, but choosing to focus on the grip and rivets gave me the opportunity to develop those skills more deliberately. That is part of learning the trade: deciding which techniques to attempt, and which ones deserve another project.

Final Reflections

This pugio brought together several things I wanted to learn: a patterned blade, a successful fish-mouth weld, and a more elaborate metal hilt. It is not a perfect archaeological reconstruction, and I wouldn't want to present it that way. It is a record of the work I was capable of at the time, what challenged me, and what I learned in the process.
